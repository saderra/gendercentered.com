Kids, Schools, and Pronouns

Schools have always taught values; the question was never whether, only whose and for whom. The pronoun fight in particular gets treated as one dispute when it's really several, and collapsing them together is what makes the argument feel impossible to resolve.

Start here

Two different pronoun questions

The first question is whether a teacher should use the name and pronouns a specific student has asked for. That's a matter of professional courtesy, the same courtesy already extended to every William who prefers "Liam" or every Katherine who goes by "Kate." Clinical research consistently links being addressed by one's chosen name and pronouns to lower rates of depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation among transgender youth, and refusal is associated with measurable harm.

The second question is entirely different: whether a school should require every teacher to introduce themselves with pronouns, or run mandatory pronoun-sharing exercises for the whole class. That's a curricular and institutional choice, not a matter of one student's dignity, and it deserves genuine debate. A mandatory pronoun round can put a closeted student in the position of lying to the whole class or coming out on the school's schedule instead of their own, the opposite of what the exercise intends.

Calling a student by the name they've asked for is professional conduct. Scripting the whole classroom to perform an identity ritual is a policy choice, and policy choices are fair game for parents to weigh in on.

Notification, books, and the classroom wall

Where the hard cases actually are

Parental notification policies, whether a school must tell parents when a child socially transitions at school, run into a real problem: they treat every family alike. For a child whose home is safe, notification is simply parenting. For a child whose home isn't, forced disclosure can precede real harm. National surveys consistently find that LGBTQ youth are dramatically overrepresented among homeless youth, largely due to family rejection. A blanket rule can't tell the difference between these two homes; a school counselor who knows the specific child sometimes can.

Book removals have risen sharply since 2021, and the organizations that track them, including PEN America, find LGBTQ-themed titles among the most frequently targeted categories nationwide. The consistency test worth applying here is simple: if the standard is genuinely about age-appropriateness rather than which characters appear in a story, it should apply evenly to every book that centers a relationship or a family structure, not disproportionately to the ones with a gay or transgender character.

Federal protection for transgender students under Title IX has itself been unstable, reversing direction across recent administrations and court rulings, which means the answer to "what are this student's rights" now depends heavily on which state, and which year, a family happens to be in.

A workable standard

Judge each of these questions by the standard already used everywhere else in the building: literary merit and relevance for library and curriculum choices, not the identity of the characters. Professional courtesy for what a teacher calls one student, separate from what a school scripts for the whole class. Clinical discretion, not a blanket mandate, for disclosure decisions that can put a specific child at risk. None of this satisfies anyone looking for a total win. That's usually a sign it's close to fair.
